**Working title:** Becoming Human Together: Sipakatau and the Transforming Work of Interreligious Learning in South Sulawesi

Interfaith dialogue is often measured by what it produces between communities: agreements, joint statements, reduced conflict. Less attention goes to what it does to the people who take part. This article asks how participants are changed by sustained interreligious learning, and it answers from a Southeast Asian context in which a local ethical vocabulary already names the kind of change at stake.

The setting is South Sulawesi, Indonesia, a region where Christians live as a minority among Muslim majorities and where Bugis-Makassar ethics speak of sipakatau, the mutual recognition of one another as fully human. Sipakatau is not a theory of dialogue. It is an ethic of everyday life that judges conduct by whether it humanises or diminishes the other. The article argues that this ethic offers interfaith studies a criterion for transformation that is neither conversion nor mere tolerance: a dialogue transforms its participants when it makes them more able to recognise the other's humanity and to be recognised in turn.

The argument proceeds in three steps. First, it sets out the problem in the literature on interreligious dialogue, where transformation is often assumed rather than described, or is described in terms drawn from Western liberal settings. Second, it presents sipakatau, together with the related notion of pacce (shared pain), as resources from South Sulawesi that describe moral change in relational terms. Third, it reflects on interreligious learning across continents, drawing on a published exchange in which four participants from different countries and traditions described what learning with one another had changed in them (EMS Einblick, "Gelebte Verständigung"). The article reads these accounts through sipakatau and shows how the categories of recognition and shared vulnerability make visible changes that a focus on outcomes misses.

The article also addresses an objection. Appeals to local ethics in dialogue can romanticise tradition or hide the hierarchies that local traditions themselves contain, including those of gender, rank and religion. Sipakatau has been used to defend existing social order as well as to criticise it. The article therefore reads it critically: it asks which uses of the ethic open recognition to those at the margins, such as religious minorities and women, and which close it. Transformation, on this account, includes the transformation of the tradition used to describe it. This keeps the proposal from becoming a claim that one culture already holds the answer, and it makes the criterion usable by partners who do not share the Bugis-Makassar background.

The contribution is twofold. For interfaith studies, the article proposes a relational criterion of transformation grounded in a non-Western ethic and testable against participants' own accounts. For Christian theology of religions in Asia, it suggests that local ethical traditions can serve as shared ground for dialogue without being absorbed into any one religion's doctrine. The article closes with implications for interreligious education in Indonesian theological schools, where dialogue is increasingly part of the curriculum but rarely assessed for what it changes in students.

Method: conceptual analysis of sipakatau and pacce in published ethnographic and theological sources; interpretive reading of first-person accounts of interreligious learning; engagement with literature on dialogue and recognition.
